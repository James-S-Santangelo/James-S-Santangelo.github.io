# Theme 1: The genetic architecture of adaptation to contemporary environments 

## Urban-rural cyanogenesis (HCN) clines

![World map of cities sampled by the Global Urban Evolution Project, coloured by urban-rural HCN cline](../assets/images/glue_map.png)
*Figure 1: Map of cities samples as part of the Global Urban Evolution Project. Cities with positive clines (HCN<sub>Urban</sub> < HCN<sub>Rural</sub>) are colored blue while cities with negative clines(HCN<sub>Urban</sub> < HCN<sub>Rural</sub>)*

Humans are having a decisive impact on natural environments around the world,
forcing species to move, adapt, or go extinct. One such form of environmental
change is urbanization and approximately 55% of the world’s human population
currently lives in cities, a figure that continues to grow annually. Because
cities are constructed to suit the needs of humans, urban environments around
the world are predicted to be more like one another than to their own
surrounding non-urban habitat, which [may drive parallel evolutionary responses
to
urbanization](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/jixm0yrdlf57rkaosme6k/Santangelo-et-al.-2020-Urban-environments-as-a-framework-to-study-parallel-evolution.pdf?rlkey=0bwo5erp9bu1mjm5yd3f60nll&dl=0).
We have tested this prediction by leveraging large-scale, replicated urban
environments to examine the extent of parallel evolution in the production of
hydrogen cyanide (HCN)—an ecologically important antiherbivore defense that
also affects tolerance to abiotic stressors (e.g., drought, frost)—in natural
populations of white clover (_Trifolium repens_). We helped launch the [GLobal
Urban Evolution Project (GLUE)](https://www.globalurbanevolution.com/), a
collaborative effort among 287 scientists from 160 countries around the world
with the common goal of understanding the mechanisms underlying urban
evolutionary change. [Our analyses
showed](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/jhoatblsbr16r6eh34u4t/Santangelo-et-al.-2022-Global-urban-environmental-change-drives-adaptation-in-white-clover.pdf?rlkey=gb6dgskpvc1sueoc3wmf50qtg&dl=0)
that urban habitats around the world have converged to similar environmental
features: cities are warmer, less vegetated, and contain more impervious
surfaces than surrounding non- urban habitats. These convergent urban
environments have driven urban-rural HCN clines in 47% of the 160 cities
sampled (see Figure 1 above), and whole genome sequencing of 2,074 plants from
26 cities has confirmed that clines are adaptive. Indeed, variation in the
strength of clines could be predicted by urban-rural changes in the strength of
drought and herbivory, factors also known to influence HCN frequencies at
continental scales.

We generated a lot of data as part of this project, which numerous projects
have leverage. For example, [Aude
Caizergues](https://audecaizergues.github.io/) used the genomic data genomic
data to explore the [demographic consequences of
urbanization](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mec.17311).
Similarly, [Nic Kooyers](https://www.plantadaptation.com/), [Brandon
Henrickson](https://louisiana.edu/directory/people/brandon-hendrickson), [Kay
Hodgins](http://hodginslab.com/), and [Paul
Battlay](https://scholar.google.com/citations?hl=en&user=QaufYW4AAAAJ) lead
efforts to examine the consequences of white clover's invasion out of the
Mediterranean to all inhabited continents, and some of the [loci underlying
adaptation in its native and introduced
ranges](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-025-02751-2).

## Quantitative trait evolution and genome-wide selection 

![Toronto sampling transect and common garden, multivariate phenotypic cline, and genome-wide XP-nSL selection scan](../assets/images/multi_cline_xpnsl.png)
*Figure 2: Top left: Populations sampled and common garden experiment used for detected quantitative trait differentiation of white clover populations across an urban-rural transect in Toronto, Canada. Top right: Multivariate phenotypic cline among Toronto white clover population. Each point represents a plant's multivariate cline score, which combines standardized measurements of 14 phenotypic traits and their genetic correlations. Bottom: Cross-Population Number of Segregating Sites by Length (XP-nSL) scores for urban and rural plants in Toronto. This statistic identifies candidates of positive selection using genome-wide haplotype data.*

Given the multifarious environmental changes associated with the development of
cities, urbanization is likely to drive differentiation in many phenotypic
traits. In line with this prediction, a common-garden experiment identified a
multivariate phenotypic cline across the city of Toronto: [urban and rural
populations were differentiated in numerous quantitative traits involved in
plant growth and
reproduction](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/h63l5tsb2ca5rho8q4eni/Santangelo-et-al.-2020-Multivariate-phenotypic-divergence-along-an-urbanization-gradient.pdf?rlkey=kgb2ov6v6ohff3wgc4rdj9vfu&dl=0)
(see Figure 2 above, top), which observational data suggested might be driven
by variation in the underlying pollinator environment. Patterns of phenotypic
differentiation appear mirrored across the genome, as recent work using 120
plants from these same populations has [identified signatures of selection in
genes underlying growth, reproduction, and abiotic stress tolerance] (see
Figure 2 above, bottom). 

## Ongoing projects and questions

We have many ongoing projects leveraging diverse techniques to better understand the traits and genes
facilitating plant adaptation to rapidly changing environments. While existing work has largely been
focused on cities, we are expanding our focus to other heavily human-modified landscapes, and to a 
broader suite of traits. Here are some sample questions we're interested in tackling:

1. What is the genetic architecture of resistance to biotic (e.g., herbivory)
   and abiotic (e.g., drought, frost) stressors? Does resistance to one
   stressor trade-off with resistance to others, or is there pleiotropy among
   underlying loci that confers resistance to multiple stressors? How does this
   affect adaptation of natural populations?
2. What is the strength of selection acting on ecologically and agriculturally
   important phenotypes? 
3. How does planting of artificially-selected white clover cultivars (e.g., by
   farmers, or in residential clover lawns) impact patterns of genetic
   diversity and adaptation of naturalized populations across urban and rural
   landscapes?
4. Are the agents of selection that have driven past adaptation to the
   environment still driving contemporary patterns selection and
   phenotypic/allele frequency change?

basis of adaptation, particularly in non-model system where such resources have been historically lacking.

## Ongoing projects and questions

In collaboration with [Yaseen Mottiar](https://mottiar.uottawa.ca/), we are developing and annotated,
haplotype-resolved, chromosome-level assembly of Eastern Leatherwood (_Dirca palustris_) to drive
future investigations into the unusual lignification process in this system. _D. palustris_ has half the 
lignin of typical plant species and flexible stems as a results, which was used as rope by indigenous peoples
and early settlers in Canada. Similarly, we are collaborating with [Carole Yauk's lab](https://caroleyauk.weebly.com/)
to develop a reference genome of the North American Herring Gull (_Larus smithsonianus_), which we will leverage
to understand how changes in pollution levels in Hamilton Harbour—a heavily contaminated industrial waterway in southern Ontario—have impacted somatic and germline mutation rate and spectra.

All of the projects described so far have used a single reference genome.
However, this can introduce biases in analyses, particularly in complex plant
genomes like white clover that are loaded with repeats and structural
rearrangements. A major focus of the lab going forward will be to add to the
growing number of chromosome-level assemblies in white clover, which will open
the door to pangenomic analyses to help reduce reference bias and drive
investigations into the structural complexities of plant genomes. 

### Neutral clines in traits with non-additive genetic architectures 

<div class="media-text" markdown="1">
![Simulated HCN clines under varying strengths of drift, selection, and gene flow](../assets/images/sims.png)

<div class="media-text__body" markdown="1">
HCN exhibits duplicate-recessive epistasis (or complimentary epistasis),
whereby plants require a functional allele at each of two interacting loci to
produce HCN. Recessivity at either locus (or both) results in the inability to
produce HCN due to the absence of one of the precursors. Because of this
non-additive genetic architecture, population of _T. repens_ may be
particularly susceptible to decreases in HCN frequencies via drift alone, since
the only way for HCN frequencies to drift upwards would be for the frequency of
the functional alleles at both loci to drift upwards. During my PhD, I
simulated the evolution of clines in HCN under gradients with varying strength
of genetic drift, selection, and levels of gene flow to examine how these
mechanisms interact in the evolution of clines in traits with non-additive
genetic architectures. We found that [drift on it's own is sufficient to drive
the deterministic loss of HCN in strongly-drifting
populations](https://www.dropbox.com/scl/fi/e43ppzkpwp043fl5r9h9s/Santangelo-Johnson-Ness-2018-Modern-spandrels-the-roles-of-genetic-drift-gene-flow-and-natural-selection-in-the-evolution-of-para.pdf?rlkey=897yvckmsumwpbt0ncloj0nvz&dl=0).
This could explain the frequently observed loss of HCN in urban populations,
which experience stronger drift in many systems. However, the strength of
observed phenotypic clines are far stronger than those generated via drift
alone, and genomic analyses have ruled out drift as a likely explanation
driving these clines.
</div>
</div>
